Standard single-wall corrugated boxes work well for e-commerce parcels and light retail goods. But when you are shipping heavy machinery components, dense agricultural products, industrial tools, or goods that need to survive multiple handling events across a long sea freight journey, you need something fundamentally stronger.

Heavy-duty corrugated boxes - double-wall and triple-wall constructions - are engineered for exactly these applications. This guide explains how they are built, how to read the specifications, what to look for when sourcing from Indian manufacturers, and how to match box construction to your actual shipping requirements.

## How Corrugated Box Construction Works

Before specifying heavy-duty boxes, it helps to understand how corrugated board is constructed.

A single-wall corrugated sheet consists of three layers: an outer liner (the flat outside face), a corrugated medium (the wavy fluted layer), and an inner liner (the flat inside face). This 3-ply construction is what most people mean when they say "cardboard box."

**Double-wall corrugated** adds a second fluted medium and an additional liner between the two fluted layers, creating a 5-ply board. This roughly doubles the crush resistance compared to single-wall and is suitable for loads up to approximately 30 kg per box.

**Triple-wall corrugated** adds a third fluted medium, creating a 7-ply board. Triple-wall is the strongest corrugated construction commercially available, approaching the structural properties of plywood in some applications. It can handle loads above 50 kg and is designed for the most demanding industrial and export applications.

## Flute Types in Heavy-Duty Applications

The flute profile (the shape and size of the corrugated waves) significantly affects the box's performance characteristics. For heavy-duty applications, the most relevant flute types are:

**A-flute (5mm):** The largest standard flute. Provides the best cushioning and vertical crush resistance. Ideal when the primary concern is protecting fragile heavy goods from impact and compression. A-flute is often used in the outer ply of double-wall boards for maximum top-to-bottom stacking strength.

**B-flute (3mm):** Smaller than A-flute, with more flutes per linear metre. Provides better puncture resistance and flat crush resistance (side-to-side). Often used as the inner ply in double-wall constructions to improve stacking strength without sacrificing cushioning from the outer A-flute.

**C-flute (4mm):** The most common flute for general purpose boxes. Falls between A and B-flute in cushioning and stacking performance. Many double-wall boxes are constructed as BC-flute (B inner, C outer), which balances cushioning, stacking strength, and material cost.

**E-flute (1.5mm):** Very thin, tight flute. Not suitable for heavy-duty outer walls, but sometimes used as the innermost ply in triple-wall constructions to add rigidity without significant thickness.

For most heavy-duty export applications, the most common constructions are:
- **Double-wall BC-flute (5-ply):** General heavy-duty shipping, goods up to 30 kg
- **Double-wall AA-flute (5-ply):** Maximum cushioning for fragile heavy goods
- **Triple-wall AAA or ABC-flute (7-ply):** Industrial palletised loads, machinery components, 50 kg+ applications

## Understanding Burst Strength and ECT Ratings

When specifying corrugated boxes, two key measurements tell you how strong the board is:


![Corrugated export boxes stacked on pallets in a fulfilment and logistics warehouse](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1542744095-291d1f67b221?w=1200&q=80&auto=format&fit=crop)
### Burst Strength (Mullen Test)

Burst strength, measured in PSI (pounds per square inch) or kPa, indicates how much pressure the board can withstand before rupturing. It is tested by pushing a steel ball through the board until it fails.

Typical burst strength ranges:
- Single-wall: 150 to 200 PSI
- Double-wall: 250 to 400 PSI
- Triple-wall: 500 to 900 PSI

Higher burst strength means the box resists puncture, sharp corners from internal contents, and rough handling better.

### Edge Crush Test (ECT)

ECT measures the stacking strength of corrugated board - how much vertical load it can bear before the flutes crush. ECT is expressed in lb/in (pounds per linear inch) or kN/m.

ECT is the more relevant measurement for export shipping because sea freight containers stack boxes under significant vertical load. A box that passes a high burst test but has low ECT will be crushed by the weight of boxes stacked on top of it.

Typical ECT values:
- Single-wall: 23 to 32 ECT
- Double-wall: 42 to 48 ECT
- Triple-wall: 71 ECT and above

**For export shipments that will be stacked in sea freight containers, specify ECT rating rather than (or in addition to) burst strength.** A box rated at 48 ECT double-wall will outperform a high burst-strength box of the same construction if stacking is the primary stress.

## Weight Limits and Stacking Guidelines

As a practical guide, here are the load ratings typically achievable with standard heavy-duty constructions. These figures assume correctly sized boxes (not oversized for the contents), dry conditions, and contents that are reasonably distributed:

| Construction | Recommended Max Load | Stacking Height (approx.) |
|-------------|---------------------|---------------------------|
| Single-wall 32 ECT | 15 to 20 kg | 4 to 5 boxes high |
| Double-wall BC 48 ECT | 25 to 35 kg | 5 to 7 boxes high |
| Double-wall AA 48 ECT | 30 to 40 kg | 4 to 6 boxes high |
| Triple-wall ABC 71 ECT | 50 to 75 kg | 6 to 8 boxes high |
| Triple-wall 900 PSI burst | 60 to 90 kg | Pallet-height |

Note that humidity significantly reduces corrugated strength. In humid sea freight containers or wet warehouse conditions, effective strength can be 20 to 40% lower than rated dry values. If your shipment route involves high humidity (tropical ports, monsoon season departures from Indian ports), account for this by specifying a stronger construction than the dry-condition math would suggest.

## Liner and Medium Paper Grades

The paper grades used in the liner and medium affect both strength and cost. For heavy-duty export applications, specify:

**Kraft liner:** Made from virgin or semi-virgin kraft pulp. Higher burst and tear strength than recycled paper at the same weight. Recommended for the outer liner when the box will face rough handling, moisture, or heavy stacking.

**Test liner:** Made from recycled paper. Lower cost, acceptable for inner liners and less demanding applications. Most eco-friendly corrugated boxes use test liner for the inner plies with kraft liner on the outer face.

**Corrugating medium:** The fluted layer between liners. Made from either virgin or recycled semi-chemical pulp. Heavier medium (150 gsm to 200 gsm) improves ECT rating; lighter medium (100 to 127 gsm) reduces weight and cost.

For sustainable export packaging, a common specification is recycled kraft outer liner (70 to 80% recycled content), recycled test liner for inner plies, and recycled semi-chemical medium. This achieves 70 to 80% recycled content overall while maintaining adequate export-grade strength. ## Standard vs Custom Box Configurations

Heavy-duty corrugated boxes for export are most commonly produced in RSC (Regular Slotted Carton) configuration - the familiar four-flap top and bottom closure. RSC is efficient to manufacture, easy to assemble on a packing line, and palletises well.

For non-standard applications, other configurations include:

**FOL (Full Overlap Slotted):** The outer flaps fully overlap when closed, adding an extra layer of corrugated at the top and bottom. This improves top-to-bottom crush resistance by 20 to 30% at minimal cost. Recommended for heavy goods where the box will be stacked with the full weight on the top flaps.

**HSC (Half Slotted Container):** Open top, used with a separate lid. Common for heavy items that are packed from above by machine.

**Die-cut and custom shapes:** For irregular contents or maximum space efficiency on a pallet. Requires tooling setup cost but can significantly reduce void fill requirements.

For most export applications, **RSC in double-wall or triple-wall with FOL top closure** is the most practical starting specification.

## Printing on Heavy-Duty Export Boxes

Heavy-duty corrugated boxes used for export frequently carry:

- Handling instructions (fragile, this side up, do not stack)
- Hazard or regulatory markings
- Brand and product identification
- Country of origin markings (required by customs in most destinations)
- Barcode or QR code for inventory management

Flexo printing in one to two colours is standard and cost-effective at scale. For branded heavy-duty packaging, colour printing is achievable but adds cost; many exporters opt for printed labels or stencilled text for the cost-sensitive industrial packaging category.

## Sourcing Heavy-Duty Boxes from India

India is a cost-effective source for heavy-duty corrugated boxes, with manufacturing concentrated in Gujarat, Maharashtra, and the Delhi NCR region. Key supply parameters to confirm when ordering:

**Bursting strength certificate:** Request the BS test certificate for the specific board grade you are ordering. Legitimate manufacturers test every production batch.

**MOQ:** Heavy-duty boxes (double and triple wall) typically have higher MOQs than standard boxes because the heavier board requires larger production runs for paper efficiency. Expect 1,000 to 5,000 units per size as a minimum.

**Lead time:** 25 to 35 days from order confirmation to port of loading. Allow 18 to 22 days additional for sea freight to the US East Coast.

**Pallet packing:** Specify how the boxes should be palletised for sea freight, including maximum pallet height and whether stretch wrap and corner boards are required.

## Checklist: What to Specify When Ordering

When requesting a quote for heavy-duty corrugated boxes, provide:

1. Internal dimensions (L x W x H in mm or inches)
2. Wall construction: double-wall or triple-wall
3. Flute combination: BC, AA, ABC, etc.
4. Bursting strength requirement (PSI or kPa) OR ECT rating
5. Liner grade: kraft liner or test liner, and recycled content percentage
6. Box style: RSC, FOL, or custom
7. Print requirements: colour count, text, logos, regulatory markings
8. Maximum load per box (kg)
9. Quantity required
10. Required delivery port and Incoterms (FOB or CIF)

Providing this specification upfront eliminates back-and-forth and allows manufacturers to quote like-for-like.
