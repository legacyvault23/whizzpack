When buyers decide to move away from synthetic polypropylene seed bags, they typically face the same follow-up question: jute or cotton? Both are natural fibres. Both are biodegradable. Both have long histories in agricultural packaging. But they behave quite differently in use, and choosing the wrong one for your application can affect seed viability, handling costs, and compliance with market requirements.

This guide compares jute and cotton seed bags across the factors that actually matter: fibre properties, durability, breathability, moisture resistance, available certifications, sustainability credentials, and import cost. Use it to make a confident decision for your specific seed type, storage environment, and customer base.

## The Short Answer

**Choose cotton if:** You are storing seeds long-term, selling into the USA or EU market, need OEKO-TEX or GOTS certification, are packaging organic seeds, or want a softer, more premium retail aesthetic.

**Choose jute if:** You are moving high-volume commodity seeds with short storage periods, need maximum load strength at the lowest possible cost per bag, or are operating in a market where jute is the established trade standard.

Read on for the full reasoning.

---

## Fibre Properties: What Makes Them Different

### Jute

Jute is a bast fibre extracted from the stem of the jute plant (Corchorus), grown primarily in Bangladesh and the eastern states of India (West Bengal, Assam, Bihar). It is one of the strongest natural fibres available and has been used in agricultural sacking for centuries.

Key jute fibre characteristics:
- High tensile strength relative to weight
- Rough, coarse texture
- High lignin content (15 to 20%)
- Absorbs moisture readily and dries slowly
- Natural colour ranges from golden yellow to medium brown
- Has a distinctive earthy smell

The high lignin content is both jute's strength and its limitation. Lignin gives jute its rigidity and load-bearing capacity, but it also makes the fibre brittle over time and prone to degradation when repeatedly wetted and dried.

### Cotton

Cotton is a seed-hair fibre from the cotton plant (Gossypium), grown primarily in India, China, and the USA. For bag manufacture, it is spun into yarn and then woven into fabric at controlled weights (GSM).

Key cotton fibre characteristics:
- Softer, more flexible than jute
- Low lignin content
- Absorbs moisture and dries more quickly than jute
- Better colour consistency (natural unbleached, bleached white, or dyed)
- No distinctive smell
- Maintains flexibility and strength across wet-dry cycles

The lower lignin content means cotton is more flexible and less prone to brittleness, but it also means cotton bags are generally lighter and less rigid than jute bags at equivalent weight.

## Durability and Load Capacity

### Jute Wins for Raw Tensile Strength

Jute outperforms cotton in raw tensile strength at comparable fabric weights. A 400 GSM jute sack will bear a heavier load than a 400 GSM cotton sack before the fabric fails.

For high-volume commodity seed applications (wheat, corn, sorghum) where bags are filled to 25 to 50 kg and handled with hooks, forklifts, and mechanical conveyors, jute's higher load capacity is a genuine advantage.

### Cotton Wins for Long-Term Durability

The picture reverses when you consider repeated use over multiple seasons. Cotton withstands wet-dry cycles and repeated washing better than jute. A jute sack that gets damp, dries, gets damp again, and dries again will become brittle and begin to shed fibres within one to two seasons. Cotton sacks can be laundered and reused for three to five seasons without significant structural degradation.

For seed banks, cooperative seed programmes, and farm operations where bags are expected to be reused year over year, cotton has meaningfully longer functional life.

## Breathability and Seed Viability

This is where the two materials diverge most significantly for seed storage applications.

### Cotton: Superior Breathability for Seed Viability

Cotton's woven structure, combined with lower fibre density, allows for more consistent air exchange. Seeds respire even in storage, and the ability to exchange air and moisture through the bag wall is critical for maintaining germination rates.

In controlled studies of grain and seed storage in natural fibre bags, cotton bags consistently produce better germination outcomes over multi-month storage periods compared to jute, primarily because cotton maintains more consistent humidity inside the bag. Cotton allows moisture to move outward gradually rather than trapping it at the fabric surface.

### Jute: Adequate Breathability for Short-Term Storage

Jute is also breathable, and it has been used for grain and seed storage for centuries without problems - but with an important caveat: those traditional applications involved short storage periods (days to weeks) and regular turnover. For longer storage (months) or where germination rates must be maintained at premium levels, the moisture-management properties of cotton give it a measurable advantage.

For heritage seeds, certified organic seed stock, and any variety where germination rate is a marketable quality, cotton is the safer choice for storage.

---

## Moisture and the Critical Jute Problem

Jute's Achilles heel in modern seed storage is its behaviour with moisture. Raw jute naturally contains hygroscopic impurities that attract and hold moisture. When exposed to high humidity (above 60% relative humidity), jute bags can develop mould and fungal growth on the fabric surface that transfers to the seeds.

This is not a theoretical risk. It is a practical limitation that has driven many seed companies operating in humid US states (the South, the Pacific Northwest) or in tropical export markets to switch from jute to cotton or to laminated jute (which largely eliminates breathability anyway).

Cotton is not immune to mould, but it dries faster and does not harbour mould as readily as jute under equivalent conditions. Unbleached, untreated cotton has minimal chemical residue that could interfere with germination even if minor surface mould does develop.

**Practical rule:** If your storage environment averages above 60% relative humidity for extended periods, cotton is the safer choice.

## Certifications: A Significant Cotton Advantage

This is where the difference between the two materials is most commercially significant for US and EU market exporters.

### OEKO-TEX Standard 100

The OEKO-TEX Standard 100 certification tests the finished textile for harmful substances including pesticide residues, heavy metals, formaldehyde, and pH levels. It covers not just the fibre but also dyes, threads, and any finishing treatments.

**Cotton:** Widely available with OEKO-TEX certification from Indian manufacturers. This certification is increasingly required by US and UK retailers and is a standard specification for seed companies selling through natural food, garden, and organic retail channels.

**Jute:** OEKO-TEX certified jute bags are available but much less common. Raw jute is frequently treated with jute batching oil (JBO), a petroleum-based lubricant applied during processing to reduce fibre breakage. JBO is not compatible with OEKO-TEX certification unless removed through a specific washing process. "JBO-free" or "food-grade" jute is available but at a significant cost premium.

If you need OEKO-TEX certification - and increasingly, you do if you are selling into EU or US premium channels - cotton is far easier and less expensive to source with this certification.

### GOTS (Global Organic Textile Standard)

GOTS certification is available for organic cotton and, to a limited extent, organic jute. However, the supply chain infrastructure for GOTS-certified jute is extremely limited compared to cotton. If your customers require GOTS, cotton is your practical option.

### Agricultural and Seed Certifications

In some markets, seed bags used for certified seed programmes must meet specific government or certification body requirements. These requirements almost always specify cotton or natural fibre without chemical treatment - a specification that raw jute often fails due to JBO treatment. JBO-free jute or pre-washed jute can be specified, but requires explicit confirmation from your supplier.

## Sustainability: Both Are Good, Cotton Is More Consistent

Both jute and cotton are natural, biodegradable fibres and substantially better than polypropylene from an end-of-life perspective. Both compost and biodegrade without leaving microplastics.

### Jute

Jute is often cited as the more sustainable fibre because it is a fast-growing crop that requires less water and fewer pesticides than conventional cotton, and because the jute plant absorbs significant CO2 during growth. These points are valid.

However, the JBO treatment issue (above) means that many commercially available jute bags arrive treated with a petroleum-derived lubricant that is not environmentally neutral. JBO-free jute resolves this but adds cost.

### Cotton

Conventional cotton is one of the most water-intensive and pesticide-intensive crops in the world - a genuine environmental negative that should not be dismissed. Organic cotton (GOTS certified) addresses the pesticide concern but is more expensive and water use remains high.

For applications where sustainability is a key marketing claim, **OEKO-TEX Standard 100 cotton** balances environmental credibility with commercial availability. **GOTS-certified organic cotton** provides the strongest sustainability claim but at a cost premium.

If your sustainability narrative centres on biodegradability and natural fibre, both materials support that claim. If it extends to organic production and chemical-free processing, cotton has better certification infrastructure to back it up.

---

## Cost Comparison

Jute is generally less expensive than cotton at equivalent sizes and weights, primarily because jute fibre costs less than cotton fibre and jute bag construction is simpler (woven sacking rather than precision-weight woven fabric).

For commodity seed applications where cost per bag is the primary driver, jute has a real advantage. For premium retail seed packaging, gift lines, or applications where certification and aesthetics matter, the price difference is typically small relative to the total product value.

## Quick Reference: Jute vs Cotton

| Factor | Jute | Cotton |
|--------|------|--------|
| Tensile strength | Higher (raw) | Lower (raw), better over repeated use |
| Breathability | Good | Better - more consistent air exchange |
| Moisture management | Absorbs and holds moisture | Absorbs and dries more quickly |
| Mould resistance | Lower | Higher |
| OEKO-TEX availability | Limited, requires JBO-free | Wide availability |
| GOTS availability | Very limited | Good availability |
| Reusability | 1 to 2 seasons | 3 to 5 seasons |
| Retail aesthetics | Rustic, rough | Smooth, premium range |
| Cost per bag | Lower | Slightly higher |
| Best for | Short-term commodity storage | Long-term storage, retail, organic markets |

---

## Making the Decision

For most seed companies and agricultural operations supplying the US or UK market, cotton is the right choice - primarily because of OEKO-TEX availability, better moisture management, and superior retail aesthetics.

Jute remains the right choice for high-volume, short-term commodity seed handling where load capacity and unit cost are the primary criteria, and where chemical treatment standards are less stringent.

For operations running both retail and bulk seed programmes, the practical answer is often both: cotton for retail-facing and long-term storage applications, jute for high-turnover commodity bulk.
