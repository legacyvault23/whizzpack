## Cotton Bags in Geological and Geotechnical Sampling

When a geotechnical engineer on a construction site or a geologist working a mining exploration programme collects a soil sample, the container that holds that sample matters more than most buyers realise.

The sample bag needs to contain loose, fine-grained material without leaking. It needs to be labelled in a way that survives transport, storage, and laboratory processing. And in many programmes, it needs to go directly into a drying oven at 105°C for up to 12 hours without the bag degrading, the label detaching, or the barcode becoming unreadable.

Cotton calico bags have been the standard for geological and soil sampling for decades. Plastic bags retain moisture and interfere with gravimetric analysis. Paper bags tear when wet. Cotton is permeable, strong when wet, chemically inert, and tolerates oven drying at the temperatures required for moisture content determination.

This guide covers what cotton bag specifications work for geological sampling programmes, why the oven drying temperature is 105°C, what happens to standard barcode labels at that temperature, and what to specify when ordering from an Indian manufacturer.

## The 105°C Standard: ASTM D2216 and AS 1289

The 105°C oven drying temperature is not arbitrary. It comes directly from the standard test methods for soil moisture content determination:

**ASTM D2216** (Standard Test Methods for Laboratory Determination of Water Content of Soil and Rock by Mass) specifies oven drying at 110 ± 5°C. Most laboratories use 105°C as their target to stay within the acceptable range and avoid risk of thermally altering certain soil types, particularly those containing gypsum or organic material.

**Australian Standard AS 1289.2.1.1** (Methods of testing soils for engineering purposes - Soil moisture content tests) specifies oven drying at 105 ± 5°C, which is the direct Australian equivalent.

**British Standard BS 1377-2** uses the same 105°C specification for standard soil testing.

The drying duration is typically 4-24 hours depending on sample size and soil type, with 12 hours overnight being the most common programme-wide standard in Australian and UK geotechnical laboratories.

The sample is weighed before and after drying to calculate the gravimetric water content. For this to be accurate, the sample bag itself must not shed fibres, deteriorate, or alter in mass during the drying cycle. Cotton calico bags satisfy this requirement. The bag is tared before sampling and the container tare is deducted from the post-drying mass.

## Why Cotton and Not Plastic or Paper?

**Plastic bags** are unsuitable for oven drying at 105°C. Polypropylene begins to deform at around 130°C, but at 105°C it still softens under load, releases volatile compounds that can contaminate adjacent sensitive samples, and can seal in moisture instead of allowing it to escape - which defeats the purpose of the test. Some programmes use plastic for transport only and transfer to containers for drying, but this adds a handling step and risk of moisture loss between collection and the lab.

**Paper bags** have a lower resistance to moisture than cotton. When collected from wet excavations or drill cores, wet soil in a paper bag will weaken the bag walls before it even reaches the lab. Paper bags also do not tolerate repeated oven drying cycles well - they become brittle after heat exposure.

**Cotton calico** absorbs moisture from the sample during transport, is strong both wet and dry, does not react with soil chemistry, and tolerates repeated 105°C cycles without degradation. The weave is tight enough to retain fine silt and sand but permeable enough to allow moisture to escape during drying. The bag loses negligible mass during oven cycling.

For organic-rich samples or samples sensitive to contamination, some programmes specify natural unbleached cotton only, because bleached cotton can introduce trace chlorine compounds. Virgin cotton with no chemical treatment is the correct specification for these applications.

## Can Cotton Handle 105°C for 12 Hours?

Yes. Cotton (cellulose fibre) begins to yellow at sustained temperatures above 120°C and chars above approximately 150°C. At 105°C with a 12-hour dwell time, 100% cotton calico retains its tensile strength and colour across hundreds of drying cycles.

The key variables to check are:

**Thread:** Cotton thread in the seams and drawstring must also be 100% cotton. Polyester thread - commonly used in cheaper bag construction because it is cheaper and easier to sew - begins to shrink and weaken at sustained 105°C temperatures, which can cause seam failure and sample loss. Specify 100% cotton thread throughout.

**Drawstring material:** The drawstring must also be 100% cotton cord. Nylon and polypropylene cords degrade, melt, or become brittle at sustained oven temperatures.

**Dye:** If the bags carry printed text or are dyed, the dye must be heat-stable to at least 120°C. Most water-based fabric dyes used in screen printing are stable well above 105°C, but confirm with the manufacturer if fluorescent or solvent-based inks are being considered.

**Closures:** Metal eyelets through which the drawstring passes are not suitable for oven drying. Metal conducts heat, becomes extremely hot, and can cause burns during removal. Eyelet-free drawstring channels stitched from the bag fabric itself are the correct construction for oven-use bags.

## Bag Specifications for Geological Sampling

The standard geological sample bag differs in several ways from a cotton seed bag or a retail calico bag:

**GSM:** Geological sample bags are typically 150-200 GSM. Lighter bags (100-120 GSM) are prone to abrasion damage in field conditions - from sharp rock fragments inside the bag, from transport in bulk sample boxes, and from the rough handling common on drilling rigs and in excavation sites. A 180 GSM calico provides the durability needed for geological programmes without adding unnecessary weight.

**Weave:** A plain weave (calico) is standard. It is strong in both warp and weft directions and has consistent porosity. Canvas weaves (twill) are sometimes used for heavier bulk samples.

**Seams:** Double-stitched side seams with bar tacks at stress points are the standard for field-grade geological bags. A single-stitched bag will fail when a heavy core sample is collected and the bag is picked up from the top.

**Drawstring channel:** A full-width folded top with a stitched channel, allowing a cotton drawstring to be pulled tight. The channel opening should be on one side only, and the drawstring should be long enough to allow knotting above the bag top - typically 40-50cm total length.

**Base:** Flat-bottomed construction (box-bottom or gusset base) is preferred for bulk samples, as it allows the bag to stand upright during filling and is more stable in sample racks and boxes. Simple flat-sewn base construction is adequate for smaller grab samples.

**Sizes:** The most common geological sample bag sizes are:

| Size | Approximate volume | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| 15 x 20 cm | 500g-1kg soil | Moisture content sub-samples |
| 20 x 30 cm | 1-2kg soil | Standard grab samples |
| 30 x 45 cm | 3-5kg soil | Bulk samples from augering |
| 40 x 60 cm | 5-10kg soil | Large composite samples |

## The Barcode Label Problem at 105°C

This is the critical challenge for modern geological sampling programmes.

Traditional geological sample bags were identified by writing directly on the bag with a marker, or by attaching a paper tag. Modern programmes use barcodes or QR codes to link the sample to a LIMS (Laboratory Information Management System) database, which tracks chain-of-custody, test requests, and results electronically.

The problem: standard paper labels with adhesive backing fail at 105°C.

**What happens to a standard label in a drying oven:**

1. The adhesive softens and flows at temperatures above 70-80°C for most pressure-sensitive adhesives
2. The label either falls off entirely during drying, or bonds permanently and irregularly to the fabric - making it unreadable or impossible to remove for batch-label tracking
3. The paper facestock absorbs moisture from the soil sample, causing the barcode to run or the paper to curl before it even reaches the oven
4. Thermal transfer barcodes (the most common type in lab environments) printed on standard coated paper begin to fade at sustained temperatures above 80-90°C

The result is a sample that has lost its identification - which in a high-volume exploration or site investigation programme can mean significant rework or, in the worst case, unusable data from a borehole that costs thousands of dollars to drill.

## Heat-Resistant Label Options for Oven Drying at 105°C

Several label materials reliably survive 105°C / 12-hour oven drying cycles:

**Polyester (PET) facestock with high-temperature acrylic adhesive:** This is the most widely used solution. PET film (the same material as Mylar) is dimensionally stable to well above 150°C. Paired with a high-temperature acrylic adhesive (rated to 120°C or above), these labels maintain adhesion to cotton fabric through repeated oven cycles. Barcodes printed via thermal transfer on PET facestock do not fade at 105°C. Suppliers: Brady Corporation, Zebra, Avery Dennison all offer heat-resistant PET label stocks.

**Tyvek labels:** Tyvek (spunbonded polyethylene) is used in demanding labelling applications including sterilisation and high-humidity environments. Tyvek is rated to approximately 120°C for short durations and 90-100°C for sustained exposure. It is borderline for repeated 12-hour cycles at 105°C and should be tested before specifying for a programme.

**Aluminium foil labels:** Adhesive-backed aluminium foil labels are fully heat-resistant (aluminium melts at over 600°C). They are readable with laser engraving or high-temperature-resistant inks. Used in high-value or permanent archival applications. The foil surface can be written on with a marker even when hot.

**Ceramic or metallic thermal transfer labels:** Synthetic material facestocks designed for extreme-temperature applications, rated to 200°C or above. Common in industrial manufacturing but increasingly used in mining laboratory programmes.

**Direct fabric marking:** For programmes that do not require scannable barcodes, thermal-transfer printing directly onto the cotton bag is an option. The bag surface accepts thermal transfer ink permanently, and there is no adhesive to fail. The limitation is that the text or barcode must be printed at the time of bag manufacture, which requires sequential numbering planned in advance.

These are available from laboratory supply companies in standard Dymo or Zebra-compatible rolls and can be printed on any thermal transfer desktop printer at the lab.

If your programme requires the label to be applied in the field before transport (where a wet sample might saturate a paper label in transit), pre-printed sequential cotton calico bags with the identifier woven or screen-printed onto the fabric are the most reliable solution. ## Drawstring Closure for Oven-Dried Samples

A tied cotton drawstring keeps the sample closed during transport and allows the bag to remain slightly open during oven drying - the drawstring is loosened at the top to allow moisture to escape freely. This is an important procedural point: a tightly closed bag will trap moisture in the upper section and require longer drying times.

Some programmes use a loose-folded-top closure rather than a drawstring, securing the bag with a cotton or paper tag through a sewn eyelet. This works but provides less security in transport.

The drawstring closure is the most practical option for programmes that use the same bag from field collection through laboratory drying and result recording. [Drawstring cotton bag construction specifications](/blogs/drawstring-cotton-seed-bags-guide) for geological applications follow the same principles as those for agricultural seed bags - the key difference is the GSM, thread specification, and the absence of any synthetic materials.

## Breathability and Moisture Management

Cotton's breathability is a functional advantage in soil sampling, not just a physical property. During transport from a drill site to the laboratory - which in remote Australian exploration can take hours or days - the cotton fabric allows some natural drying to begin, which reduces the risk of mould growth in organic-rich samples and prevents condensation inside the bag.

This same breathability is why [cotton's moisture-permeable structure](/blogs/cotton-bags-seeds-germination-breathability-guide) is valued across applications from seed storage to soil sampling: the fabric allows air exchange without allowing the contents to spill.

For very wet samples or samples from below the water table, some programmes double-bag - placing the cotton bag inside a sealed plastic bag for transport to prevent moisture loss, then removing the plastic bag at the laboratory before oven drying. This gives the best of both materials.

## FAQ

**Can cotton bags go in a laboratory drying oven at 105°C?**

Yes. 100% cotton calico bags tolerate oven drying at 105°C for the 4-24 hour periods required by ASTM D2216 and AS 1289 without degradation, provided the thread and drawstring are also 100% cotton and no synthetic components (eyelets, labels with standard adhesive) are present.

**What GSM cotton bag is recommended for geological sampling?**

150-200 GSM is the recommended range for geological and geotechnical sample bags. 180 GSM provides a good balance of strength, weight, and durability for field use with rock fragments and heavy soils. Lighter bags (100-120 GSM) are more suitable for controlled laboratory sub-sampling where field handling is not a factor.

**Why do standard barcode labels fail in a drying oven?**

Standard paper labels use pressure-sensitive adhesives rated to 70-80°C. At 105°C, the adhesive softens and the label detaches from the fabric. The paper facestock also absorbs moisture from the soil sample before drying, causing the barcode to smear. For oven-dried samples, specify polyester (PET) facestock with high-temperature acrylic adhesive, rated to at least 120°C.

**What is the minimum order for custom geological sample bags from India?**

Minimum order at Whizzpack is 5,000 units per size. Custom dimensions, 100% cotton thread specification, and printed sequential numbers are available at this minimum order quantity.

**Are Indian cotton bags certified to any standard relevant to laboratory use?**

OEKO-TEX Standard 100 certification is available and certifies that the fabric and thread contain no harmful substances. ISO 9001 covers the manufacturing quality management system. For analytical chemistry programmes with strict contamination requirements, virgin unbleached cotton with OEKO-TEX certification is the appropriate specification.

**What is the difference between geological sample bags and standard cotton seed bags?**

The key differences are GSM (geological bags are typically heavier at 150-200 GSM versus 100-150 GSM for seed bags), thread specification (100% cotton throughout versus polyester thread common in seed bags), and the absence of synthetic components in the construction. Standard cotton seed bags may use polyester thread, synthetic drawstrings, or metal eyelets - none of which are suitable for oven drying at 105°C.

**Can barcodes be printed directly on the cotton bag at manufacture?**

Yes. Screen printing of sequential numbers or fixed codes is available at manufacture. Direct thermal transfer printing of scannable barcodes onto cotton fabric is also possible with compatible equipment. This eliminates the label adhesion problem entirely and is the most reliable solution for high-volume programmes.
